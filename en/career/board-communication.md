# Board Communication & Executive Presence

A security program can be technically sound and still go unfunded. Often the gap is communication, not controls. ## Know your audience

| Audience | What they need from you | What to bring |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Board or trustees | Business impact, choices, oversight | One page: decision, options, recommendation, uncertainty |
| Executive management | Trade-offs, cost, delivery risk | Roadmap, owners, milestones, budget implications |
| Technical teams | Clear priorities and constraints | Threat context, standards, escalation paths |

The board governs; management operates. A board member does not need a firewall tutorial. They need to understand which business outcomes are exposed and what decision you are asking for.

## Translate technical risk into business impact

Use a simple narrative: **asset → threat → business consequence → decision**. "Credential-stuffing attempts are rising" is a technical observation. "Attackers are testing stolen passwords against customer accounts; a successful wave could mean account takeover, support costs, and lost trust during our peak sales month. Here are two options and my recommendation" is a board sentence. State what is uncertain and what would change your view.

Scenarios make trade-offs concrete; label estimates as estimates and never imply certainty you do not have.

## Make briefs decision-oriented

Checklist for a one-page brief:

- The **decision** needed, and by when.
- Two or three realistic **options**, including "do nothing" and its consequences.
- Your **recommendation** with reasoning in plain language.
- **Owner, rough cost, and timeline** at the level the audience needs.
- **Uncertainty**: what you know, what you assume, what would change your mind.

End with the ask.

## Metrics that mean something

| Vanity metric | Better question |
| --- | --- |
| Alerts blocked this month | Are detection and recovery times improving against our stated risk tolerance? |
| Percentage of patches applied | How long do critical internet-facing exposures remain open? |
| Phishing clicks | Are people reporting suspicious mail, and do repeat incidents fall? |

Useful metrics tie to a decision, have an owner, and resist gaming. Report trends, not isolated numbers. Service-management habits described on the companion [CSIX ITIL site](https://siedysoes.github.io/csix-itil/) can help you design measures that drive improvement.

## Bad news, crisis, culture, ethics

Bring bad news early, with options rather than alarm. In a crisis, say what is known, what is unknown, who decides, and when the next update arrives. Coordinate statements with legal and communications colleagues instead of improvising; this is education, not legal advice.

Culture is built by repeated leader behavior: admitting your own mistakes, funding the basics, and thanking people who report problems. Executive presence is not confidence theater. It includes independence — disclosing conflicts, refusing to hide inconvenient risk to protect a budget, and escalating when someone asks you to misstate facts.

## Where this fits in CCISO

Board communication sits squarely in the **Lead** domain and connects to **Govern** (who owns risk) and **Strategize** (how the plan is funded). Practice by rewriting one technical update a week as a decision brief.
